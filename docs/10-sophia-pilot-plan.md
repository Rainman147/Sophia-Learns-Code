# Sophia Pilot Plan

## 1. Purpose

Sophia is the first design partner and learner, not a laboratory subject and not a source of free product labor.

The pilot exists to discover:

- whether a true beginner understands the experience;
- whether the interactions are enjoyable and age-appropriate;
- where hidden prerequisites remain;
- whether feedback helps her recover;
- whether she remembers and transfers concepts later;
- whether the platform complements her class; and
- which features should be removed before more are added.

## 2. Consent and control

Before a recorded or instrumented session, explain:

- what is being tested;
- what data will be observed or stored;
- that the product, not Sophia, is under evaluation;
- that she may stop, skip, or request help;
- whether screen, audio, or video will be recorded;
- who can see the record;
- how long it will be retained; and
- how it can be deleted.

Participation should not be tied to family pressure, academic evaluation, or a claim that she owes the project progress.

## 3. Privacy boundary

This public repository may contain:

- synthetic lessons;
- anonymized aggregate findings;
- product decisions; and
- research procedures.

It must not contain:

- raw screen recordings;
- tutor transcripts;
- school assignments;
- grades;
- email addresses;
- credentials;
- identifiable analytics;
- private financial data; or
- psychological labels.

Private notes use a protected store with explicit retention.

## 4. Pilot stages

### Stage 1: storyboard conversation

Show a paper or slide storyboard of the first session. Ask Sophia to narrate what she believes each screen allows her to do.

Goal: detect language and navigation confusion before implementation.

### Stage 2: clickable prototype

Use scripted outcomes to test layout, narrative, pacing, prediction, error framing, and reward tone.

Goal: validate experience mechanics without runtime complexity.

### Stage 3: runnable First Contact

Use real browser Python for one lesson.

Goal: validate editor, Run, console, trace, reset, and error recovery.

### Stage 4: five-lesson vertical slice

Test the first connected sequence and a short delayed review.

Goal: validate learning continuity and the first synthesis mission.

### Stage 5: Phase 0 runway

Test the complete absolute-zero foundation with several novices.

Goal: validate exit criteria and generality.

## 5. Pre-pilot learner conversation

Ask, without turning it into an exam:

- What have you done so far in your Python class?
- What parts currently feel clear or confusing?
- Have you used an editor or terminal before?
- What would make practice feel useful rather than like extra homework?
- Do cyber cases, financial cases, puzzles, stories, or building tools sound most interesting?
- How direct do you want a tutor to be when you are stuck?
- How long is a realistic practice session on a normal day?
- What kinds of game mechanics annoy you?

Record preferences as hypotheses, not permanent traits.

## 6. Baseline assessment

The baseline is brief and low-pressure. It establishes current experience so improvement can be interpreted.

Possible tasks:

1. Point to where code would be typed and where output would appear.
2. Predict what `print("Hello")` might do.
3. Choose what seems different between text and a number.
4. Read a two-line program and describe what might happen.
5. Attempt one tiny modification.

If she already knows these, the baseline moves forward. Do not force performative ignorance to preserve the “zero” script.

## 7. First-session protocol

### Environment

- ordinary laptop she might actually use;
- normal browser;
- no developer tools visible;
- notifications minimized with permission;
- observer seated to the side rather than taking control; and
- session capped at a mutually agreed natural duration.

### Observer opening

> We are testing the product, not you. Please say what you expect, what confuses you, and anything that feels boring or childish. I may stay quiet even when the screen is unclear because I need to see where the design fails. You can ask me to explain or stop at any time.

### Observer behavior

- do not point at controls;
- do not finish sentences;
- do not praise every click;
- do not teach around the product too quickly;
- ask “What were you expecting?” rather than “Why did you do that?”;
- record exact interface language that caused confusion;
- distinguish product friction from concept difficulty; and
- intervene when frustration becomes unproductive or consent changes.

## 8. First-session task sequence

1. Enter First Contact.
2. Find and use Run.
3. Locate the output.
4. Personalize the message.
5. Predict two-line output.
6. Step through execution.
7. Intentionally remove a quote.
8. Interpret the error support.
9. Repair the code.
10. Create or update a variable.
11. Complete the tiny case banner.
12. Explain one state change.
13. Inspect the capability map.
14. Choose whether to continue or stop.

## 9. Observation sheet

For each step, record:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Expected path | Press Run |
| Actual behavior | Clicked console first |
| Verbal expectation | “I thought output goes here” |
| Emotion | Curious / neutral / tense / amused |
| Assistance | None / authored hint / observer rescue |
| Product issue | Run control blended into background |
| Learning evidence | Correctly predicted two lines |
| Follow-up | Increase control contrast; retest |

Do not reduce the session to numbers. Exact moments and quotes can reveal the mechanism of confusion.

## 10. Friction classification

Classify observed difficulty:

- **navigation friction:** could not find or operate interface;
- **language friction:** instruction or terminology unclear;
- **representation friction:** visual did not map to code;
- **concept difficulty:** target idea genuinely challenging;
- **memory difficulty:** prior concept unavailable;
- **motor/accessibility friction:** interaction method blocked performance;
- **motivation friction:** task felt pointless or controlling;
- **technical friction:** latency, crash, lost work, or inconsistent state;
- **social friction:** observer presence or family dynamic affected behavior; or
- **productive struggle:** learner used evidence and progressed.

Only the last category should be intentionally preserved.

## 11. Immediate measures

### Behavior

- time to first successful run;
- number of external rescues;
- prediction attempts;
- trace use;
- repair success;
- meaningful experiments beyond instructions;
- hints and levels;
- independent final task; and
- voluntary continuation.

### Understanding

- can distinguish code and output;
- can explain `print` in plain language;
- can describe top-to-bottom order;
- can explain why the quote error occurred;
- can describe a name/value association; and
- can modify the tiny mission without copying.

### Experience

Ask only a few questions:

- What part felt most satisfying?
- What part felt slow, confusing, or childish?
- When did you feel most in control?
- Was any help too vague or too revealing?
- Would you choose another mission now?

## 12. Delayed measures

### Approximately one day later

- write or recognize a basic `print` call;
- predict two-line output;
- repair an unmatched quote;
- use one variable in output.

### Approximately three to seven days later

- create a similar tiny program without reopening the original lesson;
- explain one error from a fresh example;
- transfer the same idea into a different mission flavor.

### Later review

Track whether the concept survives after additional topics and whether she can use it in class or local work.

## 13. Delight measures

Look for:

- leaning forward;
- spontaneous experimentation;
- laughter or surprise tied to understanding;
- desire to show the artifact;
- choosing an optional variant;
- asking a deeper “what if?” question; and
- returning without a reminder.

These observations are suggestive, not universal psychological facts.

## 14. First-session provisional success thresholds

The slice is ready for broader novice testing when:

- most core steps require no observer rescue;
- the learner can explain code versus output;
- she repairs the planned error using product support;
- the final tiny mission is completed with no solution reveal;
- passive stretches remain short;
- the tone is judged adult and respectful;
- no serious accessibility blocker appears;
- she reports at least one satisfying moment;
- delayed retrieval is meaningfully above baseline; and
- revisions from the session are recorded before expansion.

These are gates, not claims of educational efficacy.

## 15. Comparing variants

Only compare one meaningful change at a time where possible.

Candidate experiments:

- direct explanation versus question-first after an error;
- static variable diagram versus animated state change;
- general mission versus cyber-flavored mission;
- capability map versus conventional lesson list;
- small celebration versus no celebration;
- optional prediction versus required prediction; and
- two hint wordings for the same misconception.

Measure delayed understanding as well as preference.

## 16. Class integration

After each week, ask:

- Which class topic appeared?
- Did the platform make class material easier to understand?
- Did it contradict course terminology?
- Did she use a platform strategy on an assignment without copying a solution?
- Which upcoming topic would benefit from prerequisite practice?

The product complements the course rather than attempting to shadow every assignment.

## 17. Parent or mentor role

A father can provide powerful relatedness and accountability without becoming the grader.

A good weekly checkride is 10 to 15 minutes:

- Sophia chooses one artifact;
- she explains what it does;
- the mentor asks what changed in the computer;
- she describes one mistake and clue;
- the mentor asks where the pattern might apply; and
- the conversation ends with recognition of specific effort or strategy.

Avoid taking the keyboard, comparing her pace with someone else, or making every family conversation a progress review.

## 18. Iteration record

After every pilot, create a short private or anonymized report:

```text
Version tested
Capabilities targeted
What worked
Where the learner hesitated
Misconceptions observed
Fun/delight moments
Frustration moments
Accessibility issues
Immediate evidence
Delayed evidence
Changes proposed
Decision: keep / revise / remove / retest
```

Durable product decisions are then summarized in `docs/13-decisions.md` without personal records.

## 19. Stop conditions

Stop or pause a session when:

- the learner withdraws consent;
- frustration is no longer productive;
- a technical failure corrupts the experience;
- the observer is teaching more than the product;
- fatigue makes results uninterpretable;
- the task conflicts with current academic obligations; or
- the learner simply wants to stop.

A clean stop protects both the relationship and the quality of the evidence.

## 20. Expansion beyond Sophia

After the first experience is stable, recruit a small, varied novice sample:

- different ages;
- different comfort with computers;
- different educational backgrounds;
- keyboard and accessibility needs;
- learners with and without current programming classes; and
- different motivation for cyber, finance, automation, or general coding.

The design begins with Sophia. Validation must eventually extend beyond her.
